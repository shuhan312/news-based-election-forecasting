\section*{Abstract}

Rapid changes in party support make local election prediction difficult, especially for new parties. This study uses the 2026 Surrey local elections tests whether pre-election news adds predictive value beyond an adjusted history-only baseline, with particular attention to Reform UK. Stage 1 predicts candidate vote share from historical election results. Stage 2 models election–party residuals using party article share and stance balance. Stage 2 has v1 and v2 version, v2 added by-election data to v1. News-layer settings and predictions were fixed before final evaluation. Blinding applied only to the news-layer comparison. The final results showed no MAE improvement in v1, and v2 improved prediction only in some time windows. The clearest result was 31-90 time windows before the election. National and combined news reduced MAE by 0.2682 and 0.2404 percentage points, respectively. In exploratory analysis, combined news reduced MAE by 0.4948 points for non-Reform candidates but increased it by 0.6877 for Reform UK. Further analysis suggests that party differences and article volume explain much of the gain. Within-party stance had a smaller effect, but its direction was more consistent across windows. Overall, news effects varied by window, training data and party. Transfer results were mixed.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation and Research Question}

UK election forecasts have used historical election results, party support and opinion polls \parencite{STEGMAIER2023102694}. However, subnational elections are harder to forecast because polls are often limited, electoral patterns differ across areas, and the competing parties can change across elections\parencite{STOETZER2025102939}. New parties present an additional challenge because they may have limited historical election data \parencite{MONTALVO201952}.

These limitations suggest a need for more timely information. But previous research provides mixed evidence on whether campaign media improves election forecasting\parencite{BELANGER2012702,UMEDA2023956}. Whether pre-election news adds predictive value beyond historical election information remains unclear. Hence, this study tests this question against an adjusted history-only baseline in Surrey local elections.

\subsection{Approach and Objectives}

This study uses a two-stage framework. Stage 1 builds a baseline from historical election data. Stage 2 uses structured news features to model the remaining prediction errors.

The study has three objectives: (1) establish a history-only baseline; (2) test whether news adds predictive value beyond a adjusted baseline; and (3) examine when news is useful and which factors affect its predictive value, with particular attention to Reform UK.

\section{Literature Review and Research Positioning}
\subsection{Election Forecasting and Local Elections}

At the subnational level, MRP combines polling, demographic and past-election information to estimate vote shares \parencite{LAUDERDALE2020399}. Another approach uses local-election results to forecast general elections, but failed to predict both vote shares and seat distributions in 2015 \parencite{RALLINGS2016}. These studies show that information from different electoral levels can support forecasting. However, information from one level may not always transfer well to another.

Another problem is limited polling at the subnational level. \textcite{STOETZER2025102939} use information from comparable subnational elections to support forecasting. In addition, \textcite{MONTALVO201952} combine pre-electoral survey data with national polls when new parties have little historical data. But each strategy relies on information that may not be available in every election.

\subsection{News Coverage and Structured Text}

Election-news studies provide mixed evidence on predictive value. \textcite{VERMEER2024} found that media attention was associated with vote choice, especially for new parties. However, the effect of sentiment differed across media. \textcite{BELANGER2012702} found that campaign media tone did not explain errors in historical election forecasts. By contrast, \textcite{UMEDA2023956} used media reports on district-level races to successfully predict the 2017 Japanese election, but these reports were based on extensive polling. These results show that media can be informative, but its predictive value beyond existing election information remains uncertain.

To use news in prediction, relevant articles must first be selected and converted into structured features. \textcite{WANG2024} used LLM agents to select relevant news for time-series forecasting. In political news, \textcite{kuila-sarkar-2024-deciphering} showed that LLMs can convert unstructured political news into sentiment labels for specific political entities. Neither study shows that LLM-extracted news features can improve election forecasts.


\subsection{Research Gap and Study Positioning}

News may provide useful information, but some of this information may already be captured by existing election models. To address these problems, this study uses LLMs to filter and structure pre-election news and tests whether these features improve predictions beyond an adjusted no-news baseline.

\section{Data and Feature Construction}
\subsection{Election Data}

The core sample covers Surrey county-level elections in 2013, 2017, 2021 and 2026. The rolling design starts with 2013 as the initial training set, then uses 2017 and 2021 for sequential validation. The 2026 East and West Surrey elections form the final holdout. Borough and district records cover 11 councils and 46 election-year observations from 2018 to 2024. These records support local news searches and name standardisation but do not enter model training.

The study also includes 19 Surrey County Council by-elections from 2015 to 2026. Seventeen enter Stage 1, including eight that also enter Stage 2, eight used only in Stage 1, and Woking South as a separate blind case study. Of the remaining two, Warlingham remained sealed and unused, and Haslemere was later used for the replication test in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}.

\subsubsection{Sources and unit of observation}

The result of 2013, 2017 and 2021 main elections, 19 by-elections from 2015 to 2026, and separate 2026 East and West Surrey results are taken from official Surrey Council sources. Wikipedia provides the borough and district council results.

Across the 24 election events, the county-level dataset contains 343 electoral-area–election observations and 1,992 candidate records. These include 262 single-seat divisions and 81 two-seat wards. Each row represents one candidate in one electoral area and election. Candidate vote share equals the candidate's votes divided by the total votes received by all candidates in that area.

\subsubsection{Data standardisation}
To keep records consistent across years, the study standardised candidate and party names for name order, character formatting and whitespace. The pipeline retained both original and standardised names and never merged records based only on name similarity. Reform UK and UKIP also remained separate throughout the pipeline. When a party did not contest an election, the dataset recorded its participation status rather than creating a zero-vote candidate row. If a party fielded multiple candidates in one area, each remained a separate record.

\subsubsection{Validation and Source Tracking}
The pipeline automatically validates the election dataset on every generated. It verifies candidate vote totals, compares recalculated vote shares with published results, and checks turnout, record consistency, seat counts and election dates. To allow for rounding on official pages, it accepts differences of up to 0.5 percentage points for vote share and turnout. The final quality report found no duplicate candidate IDs, identity mismatches, invalid vote shares or abnormal within-area totals. The pipeline also retains source and verification information. 1,971 of the 1,992 candidate records are provided by direct source links, and news archive pages were used to verify the remaining 21 records from four by-elections.

\subsection{News Data and Corpus Construction}

  \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{pipeline_overview.png}

\subsubsection{News sources and collection scope}
\label{sec:news-sources}
The study treats Surrey local news and UK national political news separately. Local sources include SurreyLive, BBC News Surrey and Surrey-wide Guardian coverage, collected through search-engine APIs, publisher searches and web archives. National news comes from the Guardian Open Platform. Local searches cover 17 Surrey electoral areas selected under predefined rules, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:a11-divisions}. The pipeline retained only verified geographic matches when electoral definitions changed across years.

To capture changes over time, the study uses six non-overlapping pre-election windows: 1–3, 4–7, 8–14, 15–30, 31–90 and 91–180 days. It also uses cumulative windows covering the previous 72 hours and 7, 14, 30, 90 and 180 days. Across the four principal elections, the corpus contains 1,632 articles: 188 local and 1,444 national. Adding 627 national articles from by-election periods brings the total to 2,259.

\subsubsection{Eligibility and leakage screening}
Before feature construction, the pipeline screened articles using predefined eligibility rules(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a14-eligibility}). Each articles required a reliable publication date, usable English text, genuine editorial content and publication within 180 days before the election. Polling-day articles were excluded because exact publication times were unavailable.

The pipeline also filter political relevance and result leakage. Local articles had to relate to the target area or Surrey politics, and national articles had to cover relevant UK politics. Any article containing results from the same election was excluded.

\subsubsection{Structured news extraction and validation} 

The study used the Claude API to classify issue, stance and framing. During validation, it compared Claude Sonnet 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5 against a Cohen's kappa threshold of 0.60. The original stance and framing schemes fell below this threshold, so the study reduced them to three and four categories. Revised stance reached model agreement of 0.848 across 92 articles. Against human annotations, kappa was 0.741 for Haiku and 0.736 for Sonnet across 71 cases. The final pipeline used Sonnet 5 for issue and Haiku 4.5 for stance and framing. For framing, incumbent judgement and local impact met the threshold with limited evidence. Challenger emergence and voter discontent were too rare for reliable validation. The study excluded other failed tasks. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a5-llm-validation} reports the detailed results.

The pipeline validated output format and evidence against source articles, allowing one retry for failures. It also reviewed 5\% of eligibility decisions, including at least 30 articles per election. This left 1,604 of 1,632 articles with valid issue classifications, all 1,632 with framing labels, and 1,187 with stance labels because the remaining 445 mentioned no study party.

\subsubsection{News feature construction}
\label{sec:news-feature-construction}
The study converted article-level outputs into features for 12 pre-election windows at the \textbf{election-party-period level}. For each party, it calculated two \textbf{stance-based features}: party article share and stance balance. Party article share measured the party's stance-labelled articles relative to all articles in the same election and window. Stance balance measured favourable minus unfavourable articles relative to all articles about that party. Neutral articles stayed in the denominator, affecting strength but not direction. Both features were calculated separately for local and national news and used in the final model.

Unlike stance, \textbf{article count, issue and framing} were calculated at the \textbf{election--window level}, so the same values applied to all parties within each election and window. The study used seven issue categories and four framing categories. Two issue columns were excluded after a mapping error left them empty.


\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Prediction framework and research design}
Using news to predict outcomes directly would make it difficult to separate its incremental contribution from the electoral structure represented in historical data. Therefore, this study used a two-stage design, with Stage 1 modelling the historical baseline and Stage 2 testing whether news explained the remaining residuals.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.1\linewidth]{fig0c_framework.png}
  \caption{Two-stage prediction framework. Stage 2 adjusts the Stage 1 baseline using news, and compares it with a no-news adjustment control. Predictions above the dashed line were frozen before the final news-layer evaluation.}
  \label{fig:framework}
\end{figure}
\end{samepage}

Stage 2 averaged residuals by election and party and fitted a separate model for each pre-election window. As an exploratory check, the study compared this residual design with a direct model combining historical and news features on pre-2026 validation data. The residual design performed better in 16 of 18 comparisons (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a8-approach}).


\subsection{Stage 1: Historical election baseline}

Stage 1 predicts candidate vote share from historical elections, with one row per candidate and contest. All pre-2026 contests were single-seat divisions, while the 2026 holdout used two-seat wards. As a result, average candidate share fell from 22.65\% to 9.75\%. To account for differences in candidate count across contests, the target was candidate vote share $\times$ candidate count / 100. This gives each contest a mean target of 1, and predictions were then converted back to vote share.

Stage 1 used no news features. Each candidate record was represented by 35 predictors describing electoral history and contest context. After preprocessing and encoding, these became 127 model inputs (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a1-predictors}). Within each fold, all preprocessing statistics were learned only from the training data to prevent leakage.

For model selection, the study compared Ridge regression, partial pooling and LightGBM \parencite{KE2017LIGHTGBM} to represent regularised linear, pooled and flexible tree-based approaches. Four temporal folds covered 2017, 2021 and two 2025 by-election periods, with training restricted to earlier elections. Because Reform UK had little historical data, the predefined model-selection rule focused on Reform UK performance. Across 14 Reform UK validation cases, LightGBM reduced MAE by 8.3\% relative to Ridge, exceeding the 5\% threshold, and was selected. Across all candidates, out-of-fold MAE was 9.85 for LightGBM, 8.97 for Ridge and 8.87 for partial pooling.

After model selection, Stage 1 produced out-of-fold predictions for 792 candidate records across 16 time-based folds(two general-election polling days and 14 by-election polling days). Each fold corresponded to one polling day. The resulting residuals (observed vote share minus predicted vote share) formed the Stage 2 training targets. The final LightGBM model used 1,150 candidate records from elections before 7 May 2026 to predict the 2026 holdout. Within each contest, negative predictions were set to zero and the rest rescaled to sum to 100.

\subsection{Stage 2: Modelling incremental news information}
\label{sec:stage2}
Stage 2 modelled Stage 1 residuals, with positive values indicating underestimation. News features varied by election and party rather than by candidate, so residuals were averaged within each election and party cell. The analysis covered the Conservatives, Labour, Liberal Democrats, Green Party, UKIP and Reform UK.

Based on this structure, the Stage 2 training sample expanded under v2. Under v1, it contained 11 election-party cells from 2017 and 2021. 2013 served as the initial Stage 1 training election and therefore had no out-of-fold residuals. Adding eight by-elections under v2 increased the total to 45 cells and the number of Reform UK cells to seven, compared with only one in 2021. Cells with no articles were retained, with their news features set to zero.

For each news type, Stage 2 used party article share and stance balance (Section~\ref{sec:news-feature-construction}). The main analysis used combined and national news separately. Local news was used in the sensitivity analysis. Each of the 12 windows had a separate Ridge model. Because of small training set, so ridge provided simple regularisation against overfitting, with $\alpha = 1.0$ fixed before validation. The study standardised predictors using the training mean and standard deviation, and subtracted the mean training residual from the target before fitting.

\[
\widehat{r}_{ep}
=
\bar{r}
+
\sum_{j=1}^{2}
\beta_j
\frac{x_{epj}-\bar{x}_j}{s_j}.
\]

Here, $\widehat{r}_{ep}$ is the predicted residual for election $e$ and party $p$, and $\bar{r}$ is the mean training residual. The Ridge model learned how each news feature was associated with Stage 1 residuals in the training cells. Each coefficient $\beta_j$ measures the change in the residual associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in the corresponding news feature. 

Later, stage 2 added each predicted residual to the corresponding Stage 1 prediction. All candidates in the same election--party--period group received the same adjustment. Negative predictions were then set to zero and each contest rescaled to sum to 100.

\subsection{Leakage Control and Blinding}
\label{sec:leakage-control}
The modelling process followed time order, and model features used only information available before each target election. To reduce leakage risk, the pipeline also kept model features and election outcomes in separate files.

Although the 2026 articles were collected after the election, only articles published before polling day were used to construct the news features. 

Before the final news-layer evaluation, the v1 and v2 predictions were frozen, with the v2 training composition fixed in the same protocol(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a20-fitting-cells}). During Stage 1 development, performance metrics on the 2026 holdout had already been viewed, but the 2026 outcomes were not used for training or model selection. The local-news search areas (Table~\ref{tab:a11-divisions}) used pre-registered rules and publicly available 2026 outcomes before examining any news content. Hence, blinding applied only to the final news-layer comparison. The full pipeline was not outcome-blind.

\subsection{Evaluation Strategy}
The main evaluation covered 832 candidate predictions across the East Surrey and West Surrey elections. The vote-share analysis used 753 candidates from the Stage 2 party groups, excluding 79 candidates from other parties or independents. Candidate vote-share MAE was the primary metric, with RMSE used to capture larger errors. Seat-call accuracy was a secondary outcome, with the top two predicted candidates in each ward treated as elected.

To isolate the value of news from simple adjustment, the study compared each news-enhanced prediction with a no-news adjustment control. This control used the mean training residual as the Stage 2 intercept and added it to the Stage 1 baseline. It then applied the same clipping and within-contest normalisation. The study defined incremental news value as

\[
\Delta \mathrm{MAE}
=
\mathrm{MAE}_{\text{adjustment}}
-
\mathrm{MAE}_{\text{news}}.
\]

A positive value meant that news reduced prediction error.

The frozen protocol defined combined and national news across the six non-overlapping windows as the primary confirmatory family, giving 24 comparisons. Local news, cumulative windows and the 2017-only variant were pre-specified as sensitivity analyses. Uncertainty was estimated using a pairs cluster bootstrap with 2,000 resamples, treating the 81 contests as the resampling units \parencite{MACKINNON2023272}. Stage 2 was fitted on 45 election--party cells (Section~\ref{sec:stage2}), and candidates within the same cell shared the same news adjustment. Therefore, candidate-level observations were not treated as independent, and uncertainty was estimated at the contest level. This approach was appropriate because the estimated quantity was the difference in MAE rather than a regression coefficient, and the number of clusters (81) was reasonably large \parencite{MACKINNON2023272}. Each resample compared the two models on the same contests, and the resulting differences were used to construct a 95\% confidence interval.

\subsection{Diagnostic and Robustness Tests}
\label{sec:diagnostic-robustness}
In addition to the confirmatory analysis, the study evaluated three pre-specified sensitivity settings, including local news, cumulative windows and 2017-only training. LOPO was conducted after the final news-layer evaluation as a diagnostic robustness check. LOPO removed each of the five parties from the 2017 training data in turn, refitted the model on the other four, and tested performance on 2021 data for single-party dependence.

The study also checked whether news features had enough variation for reliable analysis. A feature needed at least three distinct training values for model fitting and at least ten for reported confirmatory analysis. The election-level issue and framing features had four distinct values. Therefore, they passed the fitting threshold but not the reporting threshold, so the study excluded them from the reported confirmatory models.

After the final news-layer evaluation, the study used exploratory diagnostics to examine other explanations for the main findings. Party-indicator models tested whether fixed party differences explained the improvement. Within-party stance analysis removed each party's average stance within the same training window. And this study also used volume-only control tested whether the signal reflected the amount of coverage rather than its content. Under the pre-declared rule, the study kept all sensitivity and exploratory results separate and did not promote them to confirmatory findings.


\section{Results}
\subsection{Main Confirmatory Results}
The confirmatory evaluation on the 2026 holdout covered 753 candidate predictions from the Stage 2 party groups. The Stage 1 historical baseline achieved an MAE of 4.4410 percentage points. After the no-news residual adjustment, MAE was 4.4414 under v1 and 4.4454 under v2.

The strongest confirmatory evidence came from v2. Five of its 12 specifications reduced MAE, and four had 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals entirely above zero (Figure~\ref{fig:confirmatory}; Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:a2a}--\ref{tab:a2b}).In contrast, v1 produced no MAE improvements, and most 95\% confidence intervals lay entirely below zero, this means worse performance than the no-news control. The difference between v1 and v2 reflects changes introduced in v2, and their individual effects are examined later.
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \makebox[\linewidth]{\includegraphics[width=1.15\linewidth]{fig1_confirmatory_deltas.png}}
  \caption{Forest plot of the 24 confirmatory comparisons. Positive values indicate lower MAE with news. No v1 window showed positive confidence intervals for both news types, while 31–90 days was the only v2 window where both did.}
  \label{fig:confirmatory}
\end{figure}

The clearest improvement appeared in the 31–90 day window. Combined news reduced MAE from 4.4454 to 4.2051, an improvement of 0.2404 percentage points [0.0781, 0.3866]. National news reduced MAE to 4.1773, an improvement of 0.2682 [0.1088, 0.4110]. This was the only v2 window where both news types had positive confidence intervals. The main negative result appeared at 91–180 days, where both news types worsened MAE. Hence, news improved prediction in several v2 time-windows.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Training Design and News Specification}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
Sensitivity was assessed by checked varied the construction of the news signal and check examined training composition.

Using local news only, v2 continued to reduce MAE at key windows. At 31--90 days, MAE fell from 4.4454 to 4.2571, with additional improvements at 8--14 and 91--180 days. The largest gain occurred at 91--180 days (+0.2894), showing that the timing of the strongest local-news signal differed from the main reults. (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a15-local-sensitivity}).

The window coverage also affected the strength of the signal. Under v2, combined news reduced MAE across the shorter cumulative windows. However, the 180-day combined window moved in the opposite direction. The largest improvement occurred over the previous 14 days, where MAE fell from 4.4454 to 3.8353 and RMSE from 5.7538 to 4.8741. Local news also showed improvements over shorter cumulative windows (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a12-cumulative}).

In contrast, the signal was more sensitive to training design. The 2017-only setting contained five training cells and improved MAE in 0 of 18 comparisons, reproducing the v1 pattern (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a3}). In the leave-one-party-out analysis, removing the Conservatives produced improvements in 6 of 18 comparisons, but removing any of the other four parties produced none(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a17-lopo})

\subsection{Alternative Explanations for the News Signal}
\label{sec:news-signal-diagnostics}

In the v2 31--90 day window, party indicators improved MAE by 0.8342 percentage points, compared with 0.2404 for the frozen combined-news specification. Adding news to the party-indicator model increased the improvement to 0.8504. Party indicators also outperformed frozen combined news across all six non-overlapping windows. However, within-party stance had the same sign as the frozen combined-news effect in five of the six windows, with the sixth being zero. The constant +0.8342 party-indicator effect matched the sign in only three. These results suggest that fixed party differences explain much of the overall improvement, but within-party stance more closely followed changes in the news effect across windows.

The study next examined whether the signal could be explained by reporting intensity. In the 31--90 day window, article volume alone improved MAE by 0.2247 percentage points. Combining volume and stance increased the improvement to 0.3413. Across the six non-overlapping windows, stance alone outperformed volume alone in five, with an average advantage of 0.0993 percentage points in MAE improvement. This suggests that stance contained predictive information beyond reporting intensity alone.

The study then tested whether stance remained informative after removing average party differences. Within-party stance improved MAE by 0.2291 percentage points in the 31--90 day window [0.1669, 0.2876]. After the final evaluation, seven issue and framing features fixed before fitting were also explored. Incumbent-judgement framing improved MAE by 0.5718 and was the only feature whose 95\% confidence interval lay entirely above the 0.3413 volume-plus-stance improvement (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a13-content-features}). However, only two windows had sufficient training variation for these features, so the results remained exploratory. Overall, party structure and article volume explained important parts of the signal, but within-party stance and framing still showed predictive value when tested separately.

\subsection{Reform UK and Model Behaviour}
\label{sec:reform}
Reform UK had limited historical representation in v2, with 12 candidate-level observations compared with 65--168 for the other five parties. Even so, Stage 1 achieved a Reform MAE of 3.2318 in 2026, below the overall MAE of 4.4410.

After adding news, the effect differed sharply between Reform and non-Reform candidates. In the 31--90 day combined specification, $\Delta$MAE was $+0.4948$ for non-Reform candidates but $-0.6877$ for Reform UK candidates, while the overall gain of $+0.2404$ was a candidate-count-weighted average that masked this difference (Figure~\ref{fig:reform-decomposition}). The direction of the adjustment was consistent across elections, but its effect depended on the starting baseline. In 2021, Reform's signed error relative to the recalibrated control fell from $+12.21$ to $+3.07$, correcting much of the overprediction. In 2026, however, it moved from $-1.33$ to $-3.16$, increasing the existing underprediction.

The difference between v1 and v2 was partly associated with the inclusion of Reform UK training cells. The 2021 validation setting contained no Reform UK training cells, whereas v2 included seven Reform UK cells. Removing these cells reduced the combined-news gain from $+0.2404$ to $+0.0773$ and the national-news gain from $+0.2682$ to $-0.0015$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a18-reform-attribution}). These results suggest that Reform UK training cells contributed to how the news adjustment generalised to the 2026 holdout.

\subsection{Seat-level prediction results}

Seat-call accuracy was a secondary outcome. Although Stage 1 achieved an accuracy of 0.7188, it failed to capture the 2026 seat-level changes. It correctly predicted both winning candidates in only 18 of 81 wards  (Figure~\ref{fig:seat-totals}).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig2_seat_totals.png}
  \caption{Stage 1 predicted and observed seat totals in 2026. The largest differences were Conservatives 118 vs 30, Liberal Democrats 6 vs 96, and Reform UK 0 vs 14, showing that the historical baseline missed the 2026 seat realignment.}
  \label{fig:seat-totals}
\end{figure}

The simple `elect-nobody' baseline classified every candidate as not elected and achieved 0.8053 accuracy. The four confirmed vote-share specifications remained below this benchmark for seat-call accuracy (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:a4}). The form shows seat-call accuracy did not align with confirmed MAE improvement. This suggests that news affected vote-share error and seat-call accuracy through different specifications. As seat-call accuracy was secondary, these results provide supplementary evidence.

\subsection{Transfer and replication tests}
\label{sec:transfer}

For Woking South, the pre-registered transfer rule was applied to the only informative window (91--180 days). Local news was selected within this window based on the highest 2026 $\Delta$MAE. But this specification was the worst of the 18 candidate specifications on transfer, with a $\Delta$MAE of $-3.80$ relative to the no-news baseline and increased error across all five parties (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blind-tests}). The transfer rule was therefore not supported.

Haslemere showed the same window pattern as the main 2026 results, with 31--90 days performing best and 91--180 days performing worst. Three of the 12 frozen v2 specifications had lower MAE than the no-news adjustment control (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:blind-tests}). The performance of individual specifications varied across tests. However, the mid-range window again performed relatively well in a new unseen election.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Interpreting the Incremental Predictive Value of News}

The frozen confirmatory analysis showed that pre-election news added predictive value under specific conditions. All confirmatory improvements occurred in v2, with the strongest evidence in the 31--90 day window. In this window, both combined and national news outperformed the no-news adjustment control, with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals entirely above zero.

As shown in Section~\ref{sec:reform}, the v2 gains were sensitive to training composition. Excluding the seven Reform UK training cells made the gains much smaller, especially for national news. The predictive value of news therefore depended on both timing and the composition of the training data.

This conditional pattern is consistent with the mixed evidence in previous election forecasting research \parencite{UMEDA2023956,BELANGER2012702}. These studies suggests that the predictive value of media information may depend on what the media measure captures and what information is already captured by the baseline model.


\subsection{What the News Signal Represents}

The results suggest that some information in the news features overlapped with party identity. Removing the average residual for four established parties increased the frozen-news improvement from 0.2404 to 0.3004 percentage points. However, removing the average residual for all six parties reduced it to 0.0428, with the confidence interval crossing zero [$-0.0661$, 0.1461]. This is consistent with the finding of \textcite{Titelman_Lauderdale_2025} that candidate positions and party labels can act as substitutes in explaining vote choice. This provides a parallel to the overlap between news features and party identity found here.

Party identity did not explain everything. Article volume and within-party centred stance each improved prediction when tested separately. \textcite{VERMEER2024} also examined media visibility and sentiment separately. The study found positive visibility effects, but sentiment effects differed across media. In this study, article volume and stance balance were strongly correlated in this study ($r=-0.778$). After party identity and article volume were included, adding centred stance did not improve prediction ($\Delta$MAE $=-0.1229$, 95\% CI [$-0.1595$, $-0.0879$]). Thus, stance improved prediction when tested separately, but did not add predictive value beyond party identity and article volume.

These results also highlight that news effects may vary across parties. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:reform}, the same adjustment that improved non-Reform predictions increased Reform error. This is consistent with \textcite{VERMEER2024}, who found stronger visibility effects for new parties than for established parties. Average party-level relationships may not generalise equally across parties.


\subsection{Dependence on Training Design and Baseline Error}

The v2 expansion added more elections and residual patterns to Stage 2. The added by-election cells were less reliable on their own, with estimated cell-level reliability falling from 0.9375 to 0.2253. However, the estimated correlation needed for detection changed very little, from 0.6218 to 0.6193. This suggests that the larger training sample largely made up for the lower reliability of the added cells.

Training composition also mattered. The LOPO results changed when different parties were excluded (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}), and removing the seven Reform UK training cells substantially reduced the news improvement (Section~\ref{sec:reform}). \textcite{STOETZER2025102939} show that pooling information across comparable subnational elections can improve forecasting when election-specific data are sparse. The results here extend this point by showing that the benefit also depends on which party and election cases are included in training.

The news adjustment also depended on the baseline error. The same downward adjustment reduced Reform UK overprediction in 2021 but increased underprediction in 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:reform}). Because stage 2 learns from past prediction errors. If the baseline error changes direction in a new election, the same correction can make the prediction worse.

\subsection{Methodological Implications and Limitations}

The news content covered both national and local reporting, but geographic detail remained limited. Only 68 of 1,632 articles could be linked to a single Surrey division, so division-level news features were not used. This is consistent with earlier text-based election forecasting research highlighting the importance of geographic resolution \parencite{BURNAP2016230}. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:leakage-control}, the local search areas were selected using 2026 election outcomes. Local articles represented 8.3\% of the v2 corpus. The local-only specification was used only in sensitivity analysis, although local articles also contributed to the combined specification. Therefore, results involving local news and the local component of combined specifications should be interpreted more cautiously.

Issue and framing features also had limited training variation. They passed the fitting threshold but not the reporting threshold (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic-robustness}), so their positive exploratory results require further testing.

The confirmatory analysis involved 24 comparisons without formal multiplicity correction, meaning some positive findings could occur by chance \parencite{Liu_Shiraito_2023}. The comparison structure was pre-specified, but no single window was designated as primary. The 31--90 day window was the only v2 window positive for both combined and national news, but it remained part of the 24 comparisons. Transfer results were mixed, so further testing across other areas and elections is needed.

\section{Conclusion}
This study established a history-only baseline and tested whether pre-election news added predictive value beyond a no-news adjustment control. Under v2, several specifications reduced MAE. The clearest improvement appeared in the 31--90 day window, where both combined and national news reduced MAE. v1 did not show the same improvement. The v2 results should still be interpreted with caution because 24 comparisons were tested and transfer performance was mixed.

Further analysis showed that several factors contributed to the predictive gain. Party identity and article volume explained much of the improvement. Centred stance also improved prediction when tested separately. Within-party stance showed a more consistent direction across windows. However, it did not add predictive value after party identity and article volume were included. The effect also differed across parties. In the 31--90 day combined-news analysis, prediction improved for non-Reform candidates but worse for Reform UK.

Overall, pre-election news added predictive value under some settings. Its value changed across time windows and parties, and it also depended on the training data. The study also developed an auditable two-stage forecasting framework. Future work should improve local news coverage and geographic detail.
